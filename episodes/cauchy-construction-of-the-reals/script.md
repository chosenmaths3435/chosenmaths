# So... what actually is sqrt(2)?

## s01 Hook
> visual: dark screen, a large "√2 = ?" in the center.
[b1] So... what actually is the square root of two?

> visual: the "?" is replaced by a curly-brace set. Inside it, one row at a time, two
> lists appear: (1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, ...) and (1, 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, 41/29, ...), then a
> third row that is just "...". The braces pulse at "infinite set".
[b2] Here's one honest answer. It's <bookmark mark="set"/>an infinite set. And every
thing in that set is <bookmark mark="lists"/>an infinite list of fractions.

> visual: at "title" the set shrinks away and the title card appears: "So... what
> actually is √2?"
[b3] By the end of this video, that should sound like <bookmark mark="title"/>the

## s02 The cult of numbers
> visual: a stylized Greek column or a pentagram (the Pythagorean symbol), with the words
> "All is number" beneath it. Then small whole numbers and fractions (1, 2, 3, 1/2, 2/3,
> 5/4) float around it.
[b1] About two and a half thousand years ago, the followers of Pythagoras had a creed:
<bookmark mark="creed"/>all is number. By number, they meant whole numbers, and
<bookmark mark="ratios"/>ratios of whole numbers. Fractions.

> visual: a number line from 0 to 2. Mark 0, 1, 2. Then 1/2 and 3/2 appear, then the
> quarters (1/4, 3/4, 5/4, 7/4), then the eighths, ..., each round filling the gaps on
> both halves, until the line looks solid with tick marks.
[b2] It's not a crazy belief. Between any two fractions there's another one, their
average. <bookmark mark="fill"/>Then another between those, and another, forever. The
fractions seem to fill the line with no room left over.

> visual: the number line fades. A unit square appears, sides labelled 1. Its diagonal
> is drawn in gold, labelled "?".
[b3] Then someone looked at <bookmark mark="sq"/>a square with sides of length one, and
asked how long <bookmark mark="diag"/>its diagonal is.

> visual: the label "?" becomes "√2" with the Pythagorean theorem 1² + 1² = (√2)² shown
> small beside it.
[b4] By Pythagoras' own theorem, the diagonal squared is two. So the question is: which
fraction squares to two?

> visual: a small silhouette of a boat on waves, labelled "Hippasus (legend)".
[b5] The story goes that a Pythagorean named Hippasus showed that some lengths aren't
fractions, and was <bookmark mark="boat"/>drowned at sea for it. That part is probably
legend. But the fact is real, and here's a modern proof you can see.

## s03 No fraction squares to two
> visual: a big square of side a (outlined in FG), labelled a. Next to it, two
> identical smaller squares of side b, filled teal, labelled b.
[b1] Suppose the diagonal were a fraction, a over b, written with the smallest whole
numbers possible. Then a squared equals two b squared. In pictures:
<bookmark mark="areas"/>a square with side a has exactly the same area as two squares
with side b.

> visual: the two teal squares slide into the big square, one into the bottom-left
> corner and one into the top-right corner. They overlap in a central square, which
> turns gold. The two uncovered corners (top-left and bottom-right) are outlined in
> SECONDARY and flash.
[b2] Put both small squares inside the big one, in opposite corners.
<bookmark mark="overlap"/>They overlap in the middle, and they leave
<bookmark mark="corners"/>two corners uncovered.

> visual: the gold overlap square pulses, then the two uncovered corner squares pulse.
> An equals sign appears between "gold" and "2 × corner".
[b3] The two small squares have the same total area as the big one. So the part
covered twice has to make up for the part not covered at all.
<bookmark mark="eq"/>The overlap equals the two corners.

> visual: side labels appear: the overlap's side "2b − a", each corner's side "a − b".
[b4] The overlap has side <bookmark mark="sides"/>two b minus a, and each corner has
side a minus b. Both are whole numbers, and both are smaller than a and b.

> visual: the gold square and two corner squares lift out and sit side by side: one
> square equal in area to two squares. They are clearly smaller than the original
> picture, which ghosts behind them.
[b5] So that's the same picture again: one square with the area of two equal squares,
only <bookmark mark="smaller"/>smaller. But we said a and b were as small as possible.
Contradiction.

> visual: "√2 is not a fraction" in large text, with the gold diagonal from s02
> beside it.
[b6] So no fraction squares to two. The diagonal of a square with side one is not a
fraction.

## s04 Nobody notices
> visual: the decimal expansions appear one under another, left-aligned: 1, 1.4, 1.41,
> 1.414, 1.4142, 1.41421, 1.414213, ...
[b1] In practice, nobody minds. You can't write the square root of two as a fraction,
but you can write <bookmark mark="digits"/>one point four, one point four one, one
point four one four, and keep going as long as you like.

> visual: a calculator display showing 1.414213562. Then each decimal in the list is
> rewritten as a fraction: 14/10, 141/100, 1414/1000, ...
[b2] A calculator stops at about ten digits, and that's more than any engineer needs.
<bookmark mark="frac"/>And every one of these is a fraction. So fractions are enough
for every practical purpose.

[b3] But this list of digits is exactly where you can see that something is
missing.

## s05 Something we can't see
> visual: a number line from 1 to 1.5, labelled "fractions only". The decimals from s04
> drop onto it as dots, one at a time, each landing closer to the last.
[b1] Look at what these numbers do on the number line. They get closer and closer to
each other, <bookmark mark="crowd"/>crowding around one spot.

> visual: the camera zooms in on the spot, twice. Each zoom shows the dots still
> crowding. At the center is a small hollow circle, empty.
[b2] Zoom in, <bookmark mark="zoom"/>and they keep crowding. But at the spot itself,
<bookmark mark="hole"/>there's no fraction there.

> visual: zoom back out to 1 to 1.5. A second list of fractions, 1, 3/2, 7/5, 17/12,
> 41/29, 99/70, lands as gold
> dots that alternate sides of the hollow circle, each jump smaller, like an orbit
> closing in. A faint arc connects each dot to the next.
[b3] Other lists do the same thing. <bookmark mark="orbit"/>This one jumps from one side
of the spot to the other, circling something we can't see.

> visual: cut to a dark starfield. A few stars trace ellipses around a point where
> nothing is drawn; that empty point is at one focus of every ellipse, shared by all the
> orbits. Label at the bottom: "stars orbiting Sagittarius A*".
[b4] It's like a black hole. You can't see the black hole itself. Astronomers proved
there's one at the center of our galaxy <bookmark mark="stars"/>by watching stars swing
around a point where no star is visible.

> visual: back to the number line with both lists of dots. Text appears above:
> "close to each other" (checkmark), and below it "close to ___" with the blank greyed
> out.
[b5] That's what makes these lists good evidence. Go far enough down the list, and
<bookmark mark="close"/>all of the terms are as close to each other as you like. We
can check that using fractions alone, <bookmark mark="blank"/>without ever saying what
they're getting close to.

> visual: a candidate fraction q is placed at the hollow circle. Squares of the
> decimals (1, 1.96, 1.9881, 1.999396, ...) appear beside the list, closing in on 2.
> "q² = 2" appears and is struck through with a reference back to "√2 is not a fraction".
[b6] And there really is no fraction at the spot. If these numbers were closing in on
some fraction, <bookmark mark="squares"/>their squares would close in on that
fraction's square. Their squares close in on two. So that fraction would square to
exactly two, <bookmark mark="strike"/>and we just proved none does.

## s06 Let the evidence be the number
> visual: the decimal list (1, 1.4, 1.41, ...) lifts off the number line and lines up
> horizontally in parentheses.
[b1] So here's the idea. If a list like this is the only evidence that the number
exists, why not <bookmark mark="list"/>let the list be the number?

> visual: "√2 :=" appears to the left of the list.
[b2] <bookmark mark="def"/>Define the square root of two to be this list of fractions.
We never have to find the spot. The list that crowds around it is all we need.

## s07 Too many names
> visual: the decimal list stays. Below it, the orbit list (1, 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, ...)
> appears, then a third list from above: (2, 1.5, 1.42, 1.415, 1.4143, ...). Each has a
> "√2 :=" beside it, the second and third in BAD red with a question mark.
[b1] There's a catch. <bookmark mark="two"/>This list crowds around the same spot.
<bookmark mark="three"/>So does this one. Which one is the square root of two?

> visual: the three lists line up term by term in columns. Between the decimal row and
> the from-above row, the differences appear and shrink: 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001,
> ... A tracker "gap → 0" appears.
[b2] Line any two of them up, term by term, and <bookmark mark="gap"/>the gap between
them shrinks to zero as you go down the lists.

> visual: a large curly brace gathers the three lists, plus "..." for more lists, into
> one bundle. The bundle gets a single label: √2.
[b3] So we say: two lists name the same number exactly when that gap shrinks to zero.
<bookmark mark="group"/>Gather every list whose gap with this one shrinks to zero into
one group, and let the whole group be the number.

> visual: quick flash, about four seconds: two lists (1, 1, 1, 1, ...) and (0.9, 0.99,
> 0.999, 0.9999, ...) appear one above the other, the gaps (0.1, 0.01, 0.001, ...)
> flicker between them, and "0.999... = 1" appears. Then it all clears.
[b4] Oh, and by the way, this is an easy way to see why zero point nine repeating
equals one. <bookmark mark="nines"/>These two lists are in the same group.

## s08 What a real number is
> visual: the √2 bundle from s07 returns, center. Text below: "√2 = the set of all
> sequences of rationals that close in on the same spot".
[b1] So, formally, what is the square root of two? <bookmark mark="formal"/>It's a set:
every sequence of rational numbers that closes in on that one spot. And every real
number is built the same way.

> visual: the bundle zooms out to sit as one point on a number line. Other bundles
> appear as points too: 1/2, π, e. Small "(… , … , …)" labels hint that each point is a
> bundle of lists.
[b2] Pi is one of these groups. So is <bookmark mark="half"/>one half, the group of
lists that close in on one half. <bookmark mark="add"/>To add or multiply two groups, pick a list from each
and work term by term. Whichever lists you pick, the answer lands in the same group,
and it all behaves the way numbers should.

> visual: names and date: "Charles Méray (1869), Georg Cantor (1872)".
[b3] This construction was found around <bookmark mark="names"/>eighteen seventy, by
Charles Méray and Georg Cantor. And it has one more property: you never need to do
it twice.

> visual: a list of the new numbers crowding toward a point; this time a solid dot is
> already there.
[b4] Try the same trick again, with lists of these new numbers. <bookmark mark="filled"/>
This time, every spot they crowd around is already filled. There are no holes left.

## s09 Change the ruler
> visual: the number line, with a ruler drawn between two fractions, measuring the gap
> |x − y|.
[b1] Look back at what we used. Lists, groups, and <bookmark mark="ruler"/>one way to
measure how close two fractions are. That's it.

> visual: the ruler is swapped out. On screen, without narration of the details:
> "x = 2^k · a/b (a, b odd)", "v₂(x) = k", "|x|₂ = 2^(−v₂(x))", "|0|₂ = 0",
> "d₂(x, y) = |x − y|₂".
> Then the symbol ℚ₂ appears large.
[b2] Measure closeness differently, and <bookmark mark="swap"/>the same construction
builds a completely different number system. <bookmark mark="q2"/>

> visual: "1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... = −1" writes itself under ℚ₂. Hold.
[b3] Including one where <bookmark mark="sum"/>one plus two plus four plus eight, and
so on forever, equals negative one.

> visual: the √2 bundle and ℚ₂ side by side, then fade to the channel's end card.
[b4] So the square root of two is a set of lists. And the real numbers are just one of
the ways to fill in the gaps.

## short1 A set of lists

> visual: the unit square with its gold diagonal; "not a fraction" stamped on it.

> visual: the dots crowd around the hollow circle on the number line, with the zoom
> from s05, and the orbit list jumping side to side.
[b3] On the number line, these numbers <bookmark mark="crowd"/>crowd around a spot

> visual: the three lists from s07 with their shrinking gaps, gathered by a brace.

## short2 Change the ruler
> visual: "1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... = −1" large, in the middle.
[b1] One plus two plus four plus eight, forever, equals negative one.

> visual: a red X next to it, with a growing number line where the partial sums 1, 3,
> 7, 15 run off to the right.
[b2] Of course, that's <bookmark mark="no"/>not true for the numbers you know. The sum
just keeps growing. But what if there were a number system where it was true?

> visual: a blank number line with a question mark. A ruler appears, measuring the gap
> between two fractions: |x − y|.
[b3] How do you make a new number system, anyway? It turns out
<bookmark mark="ruler"/>all you need is a way to measure how close two fractions are.

> visual: the ruler is labelled "|x − y|", then a quick montage: decimals crowding
> around a hole, the hole filled, and the label ℝ.
[b4] Measure it the usual way, by the size of their difference, and filling in the
gaps gives you <bookmark mark="reals"/>the real numbers, the ones you grew up with.

> visual: the ruler is swapped. On screen, unexplained: "x = 2^k · a/b (a, b odd)",
> "v₂(x) = k", "|x|₂ = 2^(−v₂(x))", "d₂(x, y) = |x − y|₂", then ℚ₂.
[b5] Switch to a <bookmark mark="swap"/>different ruler, and you get a different
number system.

> visual: ℚ₂ above "1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ... = −1", now with a green check.
[b6] One where <bookmark mark="check"/>one plus two plus four plus eight, forever,
really does equal negative one.
